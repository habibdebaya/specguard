Requirements analysis / Python · Z3

Checking whether
written requirements agree.

A case study of the ARP4754B wheel brake system example

Abstract. Technical requirements are repeated across analyses, specifications, and assumption catalogues. Small changes can alter what they require. specguard combines LLM-assisted translation, human review, and a logic solver to check those relationships. Applied to 176 encoded requirements and assumptions in an aircraft wheel brake example, it produces concrete counterexamples: situations allowed by one formulation that violate another. This report examines five cases involving a missing control condition, changed units, boundary differences, and component probability bounds. The results expose differences in the formal model; their interpretation depends on the source context and modelling assumptions.

The problem

A safety analysis establishes obligations that later specifications must preserve. Each statement can read sensibly in isolation while the relationship between them changes.

For example, an earlier brake-system requirement says that when one control signal is off, the other must be on. A later specification omits that condition. Both signals being off becomes a counterexample: a state the later specification permits and the earlier requirement forbids.

ARP4754B’s Appendix E traces a wheel brake system through aircraft, system, and controller development. Its linked requirements provide a worked example for checking this kind of drift.

From prose to a checkable model

The encoding retains each identifier and source statement beside its constraint. Shared symbols connect the same concepts across tables. Separate quantities represent different units; explicit axioms relate them. The solver reads the collections and comparison pairs from this encoding.

Consistency
Can all requirements in a collection hold together?
Entailment
Does a specification guarantee an earlier obligation?
Equivalence
Do two restatements impose the same obligation?

For entailment, Z3 asks whether A ∧ S ∧ ¬R is satisfiable: assumptions A and specification S hold while requirement R fails. A satisfying assignment supplies the counterexample. Equivalence checks both directions.

Entailment

A control obligation disappears.

Table E13 requires the two control signals to have opposite states. Table E19 preserves the obligation to disable alternate control when normal control is on. It omits E13-7, which requires alternate control to be on when normal control is off.

Z3 reproduces this state while satisfying the entire encoded specification. Adding the missing obligation rules it out. The result identifies a gap in the control requirements; establishing the complete system behaviour requires the architectural relationships too.

Source evidence Tables E13 and E19

E13-6 and E13-7 supply both conditions. S18-WBS-R-6109 preserves E13-6. The query checks SPEC_REQUIREMENTS against E13-7.

Equivalence

The unit changes. The number stays.

Table E14 bounds electrical-bus loss at less than 10−4 per flight. Table E28 repeats the number as a rate per hour. Under the encoded five-hour flight relation, the second formulation allows a per-flight probability five times as large.

A witness probability of 4.8828 × 10−4 per flight satisfies the hourly formulation and violates the original. The same mismatch affects the pedal-input assumption. The earlier bound entails the later one; the reverse fails. An adjusted hourly bound of 2 × 10−5 restores equivalence under this model.

Source evidence Tables E14 and E28

The pairs are E14-4 / E28-WBS-ASMP-4 and E14-5 / E28-WBS-ASMP-5. Section E.3.3, p. 108, supplies an average flight duration of five hours. The encoding uses the linear approximation p = λt as an exact solver axiom.

Equivalence

One word changes the boundary.

The aircraft-level assumption describes an overrun “above XYZ” knots as high-speed. The system-level restatement says “at or above XYZ.” At exactly the threshold, the earlier implication permits a false high-speed flag; the later implication requires it to be true.

The check therefore fails in one direction. This demonstrates unequal obligations at the boundary; the implications can still hold together elsewhere. The threshold remains symbolic in this comparison. The 30-knot finding follows the same issue to the concrete value assigned in another layer.

Source evidence Tables E2 and E10

ASMP 3.2.2-1 encodes v > XYZ ⇒ high_speed; SASP 1.1-6 encodes v ≥ XYZ ⇒ high_speed. The solver's default witness sets v and XYZ to zero, with the high-speed flag false. The same boundary difference holds at positive threshold values.

Entailment under composition

Component bounds leave a numerical gap.

Table E14 bounds each hydraulic failure probability below 3.3 × 10−5 per flight. Under the independence product axiom, those bounds alone permit joint loss above the 10−9 target cited in section E.3.11.

The default solver witness is 1.0041 × 10−9. Both examples establish the numerical gap. The source explicitly responds to hydraulic-loss concerns by adding an emergency accumulator. This comparison explains the need for additional architectural protection; the complete architecture requires a richer model.

Source evidence Table E14 and § E.3.11

E14-1 and E14-2 provide the component bounds. COMPOSITION_RELATIONS supplies their product and the target. The check is PSSA_REQUIREMENTS → AIRPLANE_CATASTROPHIC_OBJECTIVES. For valid nonnegative probabilities, the component-bound product approaches 1.089 × 10−9, 8.9% above the target.

Section E.3.11, p. 119, describes hydraulic-loss and common-cause concerns, followed by the accumulator decision. PASA-SR-12 and S18-ACFT-R-1551 capture the additional protection.

The current Boolean encoding of independence and the missing probability domains need refinement; see model limitations. The valid-probability check above confirms that this numerical counterexample survives adding the domains.

Equivalence · fixed threshold

The disagreement lands at 30 knots.

The aircraft safety assessment fixes XYZ at 30 knots and defines high-speed overrun as strictly above it. Combined with the system assumption’s inclusive boundary, the difference becomes a concrete disagreement at exactly 30 knots.

The encoded pair fails equivalence in both directions. The reverse failure also reflects the aircraft assessment’s stronger constraints: it fixes the parameter and defines both high- and low-speed states. The boundary conflict is specific to 30 knots; the two constraints can hold together at other speeds.

Source evidence Tables E9 and E10

PASA-ASMP-01 in Table E9, p. 123, sets the threshold to 30 and classifies speeds at or below it as low-speed. It cites the earlier aircraft assumptions. SASP 1.1-6 in Table E10, p. 128, retains “at or above.” Both are repeated in the master assumption catalogue, Table E28.

Encoded aircraft assessment

XYZ = 30
high_speed ⇔ v > XYZ
low_speed ⇔ v ≤ XYZ

Encoded system assumption

v ≥ XYZ ⇒ high_speed

The direct witness is v = XYZ = 30 and high_speed = false. A separate check with both constraints and v = 30 is unsatisfiable; with v = 31 it is satisfiable.

Interpretation and limits

The useful output is a traceable counterexample: a specific obligation, the competing constraint, and a state that separates them. Generic checks expose these differences once the meaning and comparison relationships have been encoded.

Source context matters. The additional failures on E14-3, E14-4, and E14-5 concern sensor, power-bus, and pedal-input assumptions. The text following Table E14 assigns these outside the wheel brake system. Their absence from its specification therefore needs allocation context when interpreting the raw failures.

The model also has limits. Probabilities currently lack explicit bounds between zero and one. Some independence statements are encoded as forbidding simultaneous failure; statistical independence allows joint failure. The separate product axiom supplies the numerical relationship in the hydraulic-loss comparison, without linking probabilities to the Boolean failure symbols.

Results depend on the translation, selected comparison pairs, and axioms, including the linear rate conversion. This single case study demonstrates useful checks of the encoded requirements. Broader coverage and a complete system safety argument require further modelling and validation.
