specguard

Formal-logic verification of aerospace safety standards

specguard is a formal verification tool for aerospace safety standards. The development and certification of civil aircraft systems is governed by such standards, which typically run to several hundred pages of English prose and structured tables. ARP4754B, the SAE Recommended Practice for the development of civil aircraft and systems, sits among the most widely adopted of these, and Boeing, Airbus, and COMAC each certify their aircraft against it. Standards of this length and structure are reviewed line by line by human engineers, but they are not historically subject to mechanical formal verification.

specguard closes that gap. The requirements of a standard are translated into Z3 constraints by a large language model under a tightly specified prompt, the translation is reviewed by a human for fidelity, and three families of generic logical checks are run against the resulting formal structure. We applied the tool to Appendix E of ARP4754B, the standard's own worked example of a wheel brake system, as a test bench, and it surfaced three real defects. Each defect emerges from the algebra of the encoded constraints rather than from any heuristic that targeted the particular finding.

Motivation

A document of 172 pages, structured into many overlapping tables and refined across multiple revisions, can contain logical defects that survive even an exhaustive human review. The table of contents below gives a sense of the surface area against which review must hold.

1 / 3

Two requirements may share an identifier yet state different obligations. A safety claim stated in one section may be restated elsewhere in a different unit, with the rewrite failing to preserve the original claim's strength. A propositional condition that the analysis treats as a biconditional may have only one of its two directions propagated to the contract against which downstream suppliers build their systems.

Each individual statement reads sensibly on its own. The inconsistency emerges only when statements are read together in pairs.

specguard automates that pairing. The remainder of this deck describes how, with Appendix E of ARP4754B as the worked example throughout.

Approach

The encoding of a standard begins with a single record per requirement. The record carries the verbatim English text, the standard's own identifier, and a Z3 expression that captures the requirement's logical content. From this small shape the rest of the apparatus follows. Symbols stand for propositions and quantities, an axiom block carries the shared algebraic relations the standard relies on, and three families of generic checks are run against whatever the requirements file declares.

Encoding by LLM

The encoding step is the hardest part of the pipeline to automate. It requires reading the natural-language text of a several-hundred-page document and producing, for each numbered requirement, a Z3 expression that captures its logical content. The combination of natural-language understanding and structured output is what large language models are best at, and so specguard delegates the encoding to an LLM under a tightly specified prompt. The prompt sits in prompts/ and is the only piece of the pipeline that is domain-aware.

The prompt instructs the LLM to make three deliberate passes over the chosen section of the standard. The first pass plans the symbol table, identifying every distinct logical concept that needs a Z3 symbol and choosing between Bool and Real on the basis of the underlying type. The second pass encodes each requirement, pairing the verbatim English of the standard with a Z3 expression that follows the structure of the English faithfully. The third pass cross-references the encoded constraints, identifies pairs of requirements that purport to be restatements of the same claim, and declares them in the equivalence map.

The justification for this division of labour is that the three roles of the pipeline are kept separate. The LLM transcribes natural-language text into formal expressions, applying a single consistent encoding convention across hundreds of requirements. The human reviewer confirms that the produced expressions faithfully represent each requirement's intent. The Z3 solver, which is mechanical and well-understood, operates on the resulting formal structure. No single component is trusted to do all three jobs. The separation makes the methodology auditable at each stage. It is also what allows a several-hundred-page standard to be encoded at all, since fully manual encoding would require weeks of expert time per revision.

What follows in this section is the encoding produced for Appendix E of ARP4754B, which serves as the worked example throughout the rest of this deck.

The data shape

One dataclass holds the three fields. Every requirement throughout the worked example, and every requirement in any standard the tool can encode, appears in this form.

@dataclass
class Req:
    id: str
    text: str
    constraint: object


Req("E13-7",
    'When "HYD 1 Enable" output is disabled, '
    'then "Alt/Emer Ctrl" output shall be enabled',
    Implies(Not(hyd1_enable_on), alt_emer_ctrl_on))

The symbol table

Boolean symbols stand for propositional facts about the system, such as a control output being enabled or a failure event having occurred. Real-valued symbols stand for measured quantities, including probabilities, failure rates, durations, and pressures. Where two requirements name the same physical concept the encoding reuses one symbol. Where the names sound similar but the unit basis differs, distinct symbols are introduced and joined later through axioms.

hyd1_enable_on                 = Bool("hyd1_enable_on")
p_loss_elec_bus_per_flight     = Real("p_loss_elec_bus_per_flight")
lambda_loss_elec_bus_per_hour  = Real("lambda_loss_elec_bus_per_hour")

Axioms

The axiom block carries shared algebraic relations the standard relies on but does not state inside any individual requirement. The most consequential of these in ARP4754B Appendix E is the conversion between per-flight probabilities and per-hour failure rates, which is derived from section E.3.3 of the standard, where it is established that flights last on average five hours.

UNIT_RELATIONS = [
    flight_duration_hours == 5,
    p_loss_elec_bus_per_flight ==
        lambda_loss_elec_bus_per_hour * flight_duration_hours,
]

Internal consistency

A single collection is internally consistent when its constraints are jointly satisfiable under the axioms. The check loads every constraint and every axiom into one solver and asks whether a model exists. A failure produces an unsat core, which is the minimal subset of constraints that contradict each other.

s = Solver()
for ax in axioms:
    s.add(ax)
for r in collection:
    s.assert_and_track(r.constraint, Bool(f"track_{r.id}"))
result = s.check()

Directional entailment

Entailment of one collection by another is checked one requirement at a time. The solver loads the entailer's full set of constraints together with the axioms, then asks whether the negation of the target requirement is satisfiable. If the answer is unsat, the target is entailed. If the answer is sat, the model returned by the solver is the witness state in which the entailer holds and the target fails.

s = Solver()
for ax in axioms:
    s.add(ax)
for c in entailer_constraints:
    s.add(c)
s.add(Not(target_constraint))
if s.check() == unsat:
    pass                  # entailed
else:
    witness = s.model()   # counterexample

Bidirectional equivalence

Two requirements that the standard claims to be restatements of each other are checked by running the entailment procedure in both directions. A pair is equivalent only if both directions hold. When one direction fails, the witness reveals the precise state in which the two formulations disagree.

a_to_b = entails([a.constraint], b.constraint, axioms)
b_to_a = entails([b.constraint], a.constraint, axioms)
equivalent = a_to_b and b_to_a

Data-driven extension

The solver carries no domain knowledge. Adding a new entailment is a single tuple appended to a list. Adding a new equivalence is a single tuple. Adding a new collection is a new module-level list of Req entries. The same solver code runs in every case, with the requirements file as the single source of what to check. The solver was not modified between the encoding's first version, which surfaced two findings, and its current version, which surfaces three findings across four additional collections.

ENTAILMENT_PAIRS = [
    ("AIRPLANE_REQUIREMENTS", "PASA_SAFETY_REQUIREMENTS"),
    ("SPEC_REQUIREMENTS", "PSSA_REQUIREMENTS"),
    ("BSCU_SPEC_REQUIREMENTS", "BSCU_PSSA_REQUIREMENTS"),
]

EQUIVALENCE_MAP = [
    ("E14-4", "E28-WBS-ASMP-4"),
    ("E14-5", "E28-WBS-ASMP-5"),
    ("ASMP 3.2.2-1", "SASP 1.1-6"),
    ...
]

Pipeline

Each run produces a typeset report. The encoded requirements appear alongside their Z3 form rendered in proper logical notation. Every check result is laid out, every witness is reported, and every unsat core is listed. The .tex file is compiled to PDF in the same step, so an artefact suitable for inclusion in a certification dossier or a technical paper is produced automatically alongside the textual output.

Finding 1: an asymmetric encoding of a paired conditional

Table E13 of Appendix E, which contains the proposed safety requirements from the WBS Preliminary System Safety Assessment, presents two paired entries that together specify a biconditional relationship between the HYD 1 Enable signal and the Alt/Emer Ctrl signal. At any moment in operation, exactly one of the two control signals is intended to be active, so a working braking path is always available.

E13-6. When "HYD 1 Enable" output is enabled, then "Alt/Emer Ctrl" output shall be disabled.

E13-7. When "HYD 1 Enable" output is disabled, then "Alt/Emer Ctrl" output shall be enabled.

In Table E19, the final Wheel Brake System Requirements Specification, only one of these two directions is propagated. Requirement S18-WBS-R-6109 captures E13-6 verbatim, but no requirement in the contract corresponds to the converse direction stated in E13-7.

The directional entailment check on the pair (SPEC_REQUIREMENTS, PSSA_REQUIREMENTS) reports E13-7 as failing. The witness state Z3 produces is one in which both control signals are simultaneously inactive.

alt_emer_ctrl_on = False
hyd1_enable_on   = False

The Preliminary System Safety Assessment forbids this state, but the contract permits it. A supplier building strictly to the contract therefore carries no formal obligation to rule it out, even though the safety analysis identifies it as a state in which braking is unavailable.

Finding 2: a unit mismatch between Tables E14 and E28

Table E14 lists the seven safety assumptions from the WBS Preliminary System Safety Assessment, two of which concern the probabilities of input-loss events:

E14-4. The probability of loss of an airplane electrical power bus will be less than 1.0E-04 per flight.

Table E28 is the master assumption catalogue for the entire development effort. It restates the seven WBS PSSA entries with a slight rewording, and for five of these the restatement preserves the original unit basis. For entries 4 and 5 it does not:

E28 WBS PSSA ASMP 4. The failure rate for loss of an airplane electrical power bus will be less than 1.0E-04 per hour of flight.

The numeric value is identical at 1.0E-04, but the unit basis has changed from a per-flight probability to a per-hour failure rate. Under the five-hour flight averaged in section E.3.3, a per-hour bound of 1.0E-04 corresponds to a per-flight probability of approximately 5.0E-04, which is five times the per-flight bound that Table E14 sets.

The two formulations are encoded with distinct symbols, related to one another through the axiom block. The bidirectional equivalence check reports the following:

E14-4  ⇔  E28-WBS-ASMP-4         NOT EQUIVALENT

E14-4 entails E28-WBS-ASMP-4     PASS
E28-WBS-ASMP-4 entails E14-4     FAIL

witness: p_loss_elec_bus_per_flight = 4.8828e-04

The witness value sits below 5.0e-04, satisfying the per-hour bound, and above 1.0e-04, violating the per-flight bound. The two restatements are therefore not equivalent. The same shape repeats for E14-5 and E28 ASMP 5.

The asymmetry in the rewrite is the signature of a transcription event. Five of the seven entries were carried through unchanged, while two had their units flipped without an accompanying numeric adjustment. A unit-preserving restatement would have produced two formulations that Z3 could prove equivalent. The actual restatement does not.

Finding 3: a strict-versus-non-strict drift between AFHA and SFHA

Table E2 of Appendix E lists the eight assumptions of the Aircraft Functional Hazard Assessment, which are inputs to the airplane-level safety analysis. Among these is a definition of what constitutes a high-speed runway overrun:

ASMP 3.2.2-1. Overrunning the runway length above "XYZ" knots is considered a high speed overrun.

Table E10, the assumption catalogue of the System Functional Hazard Assessment one layer below, restates the same definition with slightly different wording:

SASP 1.1-6. Overrunning the runway length at or above "XYZ" knots is considered a high speed overrun.

The two statements differ only in the inclusivity of the inequality. The AFHA version uses "above", a strict comparison. The SFHA version uses "at or above", a non-strict comparison. PASA-ASMP-01, which appears later in the standard, fixes the parametric XYZ at 30 knots and uses "above 30" in the strict form, aligning with the AFHA wording.

Encoded faithfully with shared symbols for the groundspeed and the threshold, the bidirectional equivalence check on the pair reports the following:

ASMP 3.2.2-1  ⇔  SASP 1.1-6      NOT EQUIVALENT

ASMP 3.2.2-1 entails SASP 1.1-6  FAIL
SASP 1.1-6 entails ASMP 3.2.2-1  PASS

witness: groundspeed_overrun_kt = xyz_overrun_threshold_kt
         high_speed_overrun     = False

The witness state has the groundspeed exactly equal to the threshold. At that boundary the SFHA wording classifies the overrun as high-speed, while the AFHA wording does not. The two restatements of what was intended to be a single definition are therefore not equivalent under the algebra of the encoded constraints. The drift fits the same shape as the unit mismatch above, namely a transcription event between two layers of the analysis, here flipping a single inequality direction rather than a unit basis.

Implications

The most direct implication of specguard is that an aerospace safety standard can be verified algebraically rather than by exhaustive line-by-line review. The verification cost of a several-hundred-page document drops from hundreds of engineer-hours per revision to a single solver run against a translated requirements file. The translation itself, which previously required a human typing for weeks, is now performed by an LLM in minutes and confirmed by a human in hours. The economics of safety verification therefore shifts substantially. Full algebraic checking becomes feasible at every revision, every release, and every transcription of a claim from one layer of analysis to another.

The three findings produced by running specguard against Appendix E of ARP4754B are concrete evidence of what this looks like in practice. Each one is a restatement of the same underlying claim across two parts of the standard, in which the restatement fails to preserve the claim under a transformation that human review does not perform. Finding 1 fails to preserve a biconditional, because only one of its two directions is propagated to the supplier-facing specification. Finding 2 fails to preserve a probability bound, because the unit basis is changed without an accompanying numeric adjustment. Finding 3 fails to preserve a definition, because the boundary inequality is altered between the airplane and system layers of analysis.

The shape is structural to layered safety standards rather than incidental to ARP4754B. Aerospace standards in general organise their requirements into a Preliminary System Safety Assessment and a downstream specification across multiple system layers. They mix per-flight probabilities with per-hour failure rates. They restate upstream claims into the contracts against which suppliers build their systems. Each of these conventions creates the surface area on which the kind of restatement drift specguard surfaces becomes possible. The methodology generalises directly to other standards in this domain.
